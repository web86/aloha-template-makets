Night snorkeling with manta rays in Kona

By Dmitry, Head of Guest Experience · Updated October 2026 · 8 min read

In this guide

You float on the surface, holding a lit board. Plankton swirls in the beam. Then a manta ray the width of a car rises out of the dark, opens its mouth and somersaults a foot below you. For many of our guests it’s the best hour of the whole trip.

Short version: book a small-group night snorkel from Keauhou Bay or Honokōhau Harbor, about $100–$170 per person. Sighting rates are above 90% year-round, highest April to October. From Mauna Lani it’s a [35–50] minute drive.

What it’s like

Tours leave around sunset. The boat ride is short, from five minutes to half an hour depending on the harbor. You get a wetsuit top, mask and fins, then slip into the water and hold onto a floating light board with the group. You don’t swim much; you float and watch.

The lights pull in plankton, and the plankton pulls in the mantas. Kona’s reef mantas are gentle filter feeders with no stinger. Some have been visiting the same spots for years, and local researchers know them by name.

Where the mantas are

Two sites on the Kona coast work almost every night. Most tours go to one of them.

Prices and booking

  • Shared snorkel tour: about $100–$170 per person, plus taxes and harbor fees.
  • Ride-along seat (stay on the boat): about $70–$100.
  • Private charter: from about $1,050 for the boat.
  • Book 3–6 weeks ahead in summer and holidays. Most operators offer a free re-ride if no mantas show.

When to go

Mantas feed here all year. April to October usually brings calmer water and the best odds. Winter swells can cancel a night; plan it early in your trip so there’s room to reschedule.

What to bring

  • Swimsuit under your clothes, a towel and a warm layer for the ride back
  • Motion-sickness tablets if you need them, taken an hour before
  • No sunscreen or lotion: it’s dark, and the gear grips better on dry skin
  • Prescription mask if you wear glasses (ask the operator)

Mantas: look, don’t touch

Keep your hands on the board and your legs up. Touching removes the protective coating on a manta’s skin, and Hawaii law protects them. Let them come to you; they will.
